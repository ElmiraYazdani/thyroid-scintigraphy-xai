An Explainable Deep Learning Mixture‑of‑Experts Framework with GAN‑Based Data Augmentation for Four‑Class Classification of Thyroid Disease Using [99mTc]Tc‑Pertechnetate Scintigraphy: A Multicenter Study

Abstract

Summary

Introduction: Thyroid scintigraphy provides valuable functional information for evaluating thyroid disorders; however, interpretation remains dependent on expert assessment and is challenged by heterogeneous disease patterns and overlapping scintigraphic appearances. Although deep learning (DL) models have shown potential for automated thyroid image analysis, conventional multiclass classifiers rely on shared representations and provide limited interpretability. This study aimed to develop an explainable class-specific Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) framework for multiclass thyroid scintigraphy classification and investigate expert specialization using explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) in a multicenter setting.

Methods: A retrospective dataset of [99mTc]Tc‑pertechnetate thyroid scintigraphy images collected from five nuclear medicine centers was analyzed. Images were categorized into four diagnostic groups: diffuse goiter (n = 691), tumoral disease (n = 922), thyroiditis (n = 423), and normal scintigraphy (n = 337). A preprocessing pipeline including denoising, deblurring, contrast enhancement, resizing, normalization, and training-only augmentation was applied. Conditional GAN-based augmentation was used to address class imbalance, resulting in a final dataset of 3,526 images. Five CNN architectures were evaluated as baseline models. The proposed MoE framework consisted of four class-specific expert networks trained using a one-versus-rest strategy, followed by a trainable fusion network. Model interpretation was performed using Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) and expert-response analysis. A binary tumoral versus non-tumoral classification experiment was additionally performed to evaluate the effect of reducing diagnostic complexity.

Results: Among baseline CNN models, DenseNet121 achieved the highest accuracy (0.674 ± 0.023), while MobileNetV2 achieved the highest macro-F1 score (0.640 ± 0.014) and ROC-AUC (0.853 ± 0.016). The MoE framework demonstrated comparable multiclass performance, with the VGG11-based MoE achieving the highest fusion accuracy (0.676 ± 0.021). The corresponding expert accuracies were 0.786 ± 0.026, 0.786 ± 0.016, 0.950 ± 0.013, and 0.866 ± 0.012 for diffuse goiter, tumoral disease, thyroiditis, and normal scintigraphy experts, respectively. Grad-CAM visualization demonstrated that predictions were mainly associated with clinically relevant thyroid uptake regions. Binary classification further improved performance, highlighting the impact of diagnostic granularity on classification difficulty.

Conclusion: This study introduces an explainable class-specific MoE framework for multiclass thyroid scintigraphy analysis in a heterogeneous multicenter setting. By enabling specialized learning pathways for different diagnostic categories and providing interpretable expert-level decision analysis, the proposed approach addresses important limitations of conventional CNN-based classification. The findings demonstrate the potential of MoE and XAI strategies to improve transparency and reliability of AI-assisted nuclear medicine image interpretation. Further external validation on independent multicenter cohorts is required before clinical translation.

Method

Class-specific experts under one common protocol

A total of 2,373 planar [99mTc]Tc‑pertechnetate thyroid scintigraphy images were retrospectively collected from five nuclear medicine centers and assigned to four diagnostic groups — diffuse goiter (691), tumoral disease (922), thyroiditis (423), and normal scintigraphy (337). Images were denoised with SwinIR (noise level 25), deblurred with NAFNet, contrast-enhanced with CLAHE (clip limit 2.0, 8×8 tiles), resized to 128×128 pixels, and normalized to [0, 1]. Class imbalance was addressed with a class-conditional GAN, bringing every group up to 822 training images and yielding a final corpus of 3,526 images. Baseline CNN and MoE frameworks were evaluated under an identical stratified five-fold cross-validation protocol (fixed seed 42, ~80/20 train/validation split per fold, shared fold assignments) so that differences reflect modeling choices rather than data partitioning. All synthetic and augmented images were confined to the training partition.

Multicenter acquisition characteristics

The five contributing centers differed in gamma-camera manufacturer and model, collimator configuration, native matrix, pixel size, zoom, and energy window. These differences were deliberately retained in the source data to preserve real-world multicenter heterogeneity; standardization to a common 128×128 input happened only at the preprocessing stage.

Component 1

Conditional GAN augmentation

A single class-conditional DCGAN — trained jointly across all four groups rather than one GAN per group — synthesizes additional training images. Hinge adversarial loss, spectral normalization in the discriminator, differentiable augmentation, and a mode-seeking regularizer keep training stable and prevent the generator from collapsing to one canonical appearance per group.

Component 2

Baseline CNNs

Five established architectures — VGG11, MobileNetV2, ResNet18, EfficientNet-B0, and DenseNet121 — applied to the single-channel grayscale image. Each was trained for up to 100 epochs per fold with an initial learning rate of 1×10−3, batch size 32, and a cosine-annealing schedule (Tmax = 100, ηmin = 1×10−6).

Component 3

Class-specific Mixture-of-Experts

Four lightweight one-versus-rest expert CNNs, one per diagnostic class, are trained independently; their frozen positive-class logits are concatenated with an image embedding from a separate trainable fusion backbone and passed to a trainable fusion MLP (db + 4 → 256 → 64 → 4) for the final four-class decision. Unlike sparse-routing MoE, every expert contributes to every prediction.

GAN training configuration

Generator diversity was monitored quantitatively rather than by visual inspection: every 25 epochs, the mean pairwise L1 pixel distance among freshly generated same-class samples was compared against the corresponding real-data diversity, and checkpoints where any group fell below 15% of its real-data baseline were rejected in favor of an earlier stable checkpoint.

Mixture-of-Experts architecture

Each 128×128 grayscale image is replicated across three identical channels to match the expert input format. Each expert is an ImageNet-pretrained backbone — DenseNet121, MobileNetV2, or VGG11, one architecture per run and shared by all four experts — whose original final classification layer is replaced by a Linear(db → 2) head producing the negative- and positive-class logits of its binary task. A separate backbone of the same architecture, with its classification head discarded, serves as the trainable fusion feature extractor. Training proceeds in two stages per fold: experts are trained independently (AdamW, up to 100 iterations, learning rate 1×10−4, batch size 16, ReduceLROnPlateau, gradient-norm clipping at 1, with minority oversampling and up to ±45° rotation of the duplicated samples), then frozen while the fusion backbone and fusion MLP are trained for up to 50 epochs (batch size 16, learning rate 1×10−3) with four-class cross-entropy.

Figure 2. Architecture and inference pipeline of the Mixture-of-Experts framework. The 128×128×3 input, formed by replicating the grayscale image across three channels, is passed both to four frozen class-specific experts and to a separate trainable fusion feature extractor. Each expert is an ImageNet-pretrained backbone (DenseNet121, MobileNetV2, or VGG11 — one architecture per run) whose original final classification layer is replaced by Linear(db → 2), and contributes its positive-class logit sk(X) = [zk(X)]1, giving the expert vector v(X) ∈ ℝ4. The fusion feature extractor uses the same backbone architecture with its classification head discarded and yields an image embedding φb(X) ∈ ℝdb (db = 1,024, 1,280, or 4,096). The two are concatenated into u(X) = [φb(X)T, v(X)T]T ∈ ℝdb+4 and mapped by the fusion MLP (db + 4 → 256 → 64 → 4, ReLU, dropout p = 0.3) to the output logits o(X) ∈ ℝ4; the predicted class is ŷ(X) = argmaxk [o(X)]k. Training is two-stage: the experts are trained independently with a one-versus-rest two-class cross-entropy loss (Stage 1), then held frozen while the fusion backbone and MLP are trained with four-class cross-entropy (Stage 2).
Results

Comparable accuracy, far better interpretability

The MoE framework matched the strongest baseline CNN on overall multiclass accuracy while decomposing the decision into four class-specific expert responses that can be inspected directly. Across every framework, thyroiditis was the most separable diagnostic category and normal scintigraphy the hardest.

Dataset distribution

Center contributions were highly uneven, and all four diagnostic categories were represented at every center. The GAN-based augmentation strategy brought each class up to 822 training images (3,288 in the balanced GAN training distribution), giving a final augmented corpus of 3,526 images.

Baseline CNN classification

Differences among the five baseline architectures were modest. DenseNet121 reached the highest mean accuracy (0.674 ± 0.023), while MobileNetV2 led on macro-precision, macro-recall, macro-F1, and ROC-AUC, and also showed the lowest fold-to-fold variability. ResNet18 had the lowest macro-F1 (0.615 ± 0.057) and the greatest variability in recall and F1.

Mixture-of-Experts framework

The MoE framework was evaluated with DenseNet121, MobileNetV2, and VGG11 as CNN backbones under the same five-fold protocol. Expert behavior was highly consistent across backbones: the thyroiditis expert reached 0.950–0.954 validation accuracy and the normal expert 0.861–0.866, while the diffuse-goiter and tumoral experts stayed in the 0.781–0.793 range. At the fusion level, VGG11-MoE achieved the highest overall accuracy (0.676), ahead of DenseNet121-MoE (0.671) and MobileNetV2-MoE (0.662).

What each expert learned

Because the fusion network consumes four interpretable positive-class logits alongside the image embedding, the contribution of each expert can be read off directly. Pooled across folds, the thyroiditis expert shows dominant positive activation on true thyroiditis cases and the tumoral expert responds strongly to tumoral samples; separation is weaker for diffuse goiter and normal, mirroring their lower class-wise discrimination. The consistency of this pattern across all three backbones indicates the fusion stage preserved expert diversity rather than collapsing onto a single dominant expert.

Explainability with Grad-CAM

Grad-CAM attention concentrated on thyroid uptake regions rather than image background, indicating that the networks learned physiologically relevant features. For the baseline DenseNet121, correctly classified cases show attention focused around the thyroid, whereas misclassified examples show attention distributed more broadly or shifted away from the gland. The MoE experts produced markedly more localized, class-specific activation maps, a direct consequence of each expert being trained on its own one-versus-rest task. Effect of classification granularity

Regrouping the four diagnostic categories into tumoral versus non-tumoral improved discrimination for every architecture. DenseNet121 was the strongest overall, with the highest AUC (0.843 ± 0.029), sensitivity (0.683 ± 0.050), balanced accuracy (0.776 ± 0.022), and tumoral F1-score (0.722 ± 0.030); VGG11 matched its accuracy (0.798) with the highest specificity (0.902 ± 0.024). Across all architectures specificity exceeded sensitivity, meaning the models were more consistent at excluding non-tumoral cases than at detecting every tumoral one.

Conclusion

Clinical takeaway

This study developed and systematically evaluated an explainable deep learning framework for four-class classification of thyroid scintigraphy in a heterogeneous multicenter setting. By placing class-specific expert networks inside a Mixture-of-Experts architecture, the proposed approach models distinct diagnostic patterns instead of forcing a single unified classifier to represent all of them; the individual experts learned meaningful specialization behaviors, and integrating them through a fusion network produced a balanced multiclass prediction strategy at accuracy comparable to the best conventional CNN baseline.

The findings also underline how difficult automated thyroid scintigraphy interpretation remains. Although the models achieved promising overall discrimination, performance varied substantially across diagnostic categories — thyroiditis was consistently well classified while normal scintigraphy remained the hardest — so accuracy-based evaluation alone does not fully represent clinical applicability. Grad-CAM visualization and expert-response analysis improved transparency by exposing both the image regions and the decision patterns behind each prediction, and the multicenter design, retained acquisition variability, and planned release of the dataset and implementation support reproducibility and future benchmarking.

Overall, the proposed explainable MoE framework is a step toward robust AI-assisted thyroid scintigraphy interpretation, but the current models should be treated as decision-support systems rather than replacements for specialist evaluation. Independent external validation, prospective clinical testing, and integration with complementary clinical information are warranted before any role in routine nuclear medicine practice.

Data & Code Availability

Availability

The de-identified thyroid scintigraphy dataset analyzed in this study, together with the source code required to reproduce the preprocessing pipeline, model development, training procedures, and the main experimental analyses, will be publicly released upon publication of this article on this project page. The release will contain the thyroid scintigraphy images and corresponding diagnostic labels, documentation of the original four-class diagnostic scheme used for model development, and implementations of the baseline CNN and Mixture-of-Experts frameworks, along with configuration files and instructions for reproducing the reported experiments.

The source code is available in the GitHub repository. The de-identified dataset will be published in the future — check back, or watch the repository, for the release.
