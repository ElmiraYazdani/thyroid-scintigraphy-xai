An Explainable Deep Learning Mixture‑of‑Experts Framework with GAN‑Based Data Augmentation for Four‑Class Classification of Thyroid Disease Using [99mTc]Tc‑Pertechnetate Scintigraphy: A Multicenter Study

Abstract

Summary

Purpose: Thyroid scintigraphy provides valuable functional information for evaluating thyroid disorders; however, interpretation remains dependent on expert assessment and is challenged by heterogeneous disease patterns and overlapping scintigraphic appearances. Although deep learning (DL) models have shown potential for automated thyroid image analysis, conventional multiclass classifiers rely on shared representations and provide limited interpretability. This study aimed to develop an explainable class-specific Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) framework for multiclass thyroid scintigraphy classification and investigate expert specialization using explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) in a multicenter setting.

Methods: A retrospective dataset of [99mTc]Tc‑pertechnetate thyroid scintigraphy images collected from five nuclear medicine centers was analyzed. Images were categorized into four diagnostic groups: diffuse goiter (n = 691), tumoral disease (n = 922), thyroiditis (n = 423), and normal scintigraphy (n = 337). A preprocessing pipeline including denoising, deblurring, contrast enhancement, resizing, normalization, and training-only augmentation was applied. Conditional GAN-based augmentation was used to address class imbalance, resulting in a final dataset of 3,526 images. Five CNN architectures were evaluated as baseline models. The proposed MoE framework consisted of four class-specific expert networks trained using a one-versus-rest strategy, followed by a trainable fusion network. Model interpretation was performed using Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) and expert-response analysis. A binary tumoral versus non-tumoral classification experiment was additionally performed to evaluate the effect of reducing diagnostic complexity.

Results: The MoE framework demonstrated comparable multiclass performance to baseline CNN models, with the VGG11-based MoE achieving the highest fusion accuracy (0.676 ± 0.021). The corresponding expert accuracies were 0.786 ± 0.026, 0.786 ± 0.016, 0.950 ± 0.013, and 0.866 ± 0.012 for diffuse goiter, tumoral disease, thyroiditis, and normal scintigraphy experts, respectively. Grad-CAM visualization demonstrated that predictions were mainly associated with clinically relevant thyroid uptake regions. Binary classification further improved performance, highlighting the impact of diagnostic granularity on classification difficulty.

Conclusion: This study introduces an explainable class-specific MoE framework for multiclass thyroid scintigraphy analysis in a heterogeneous multicenter setting. By enabling specialized learning pathways for different diagnostic categories and providing interpretable expert-level decision analysis, the proposed approach addresses important limitations of conventional CNN-based classification. The findings demonstrate the potential of MoE and XAI strategies to improve transparency and interpretability of AI-assisted nuclear medicine image interpretation. Further external validation on independent multicenter cohorts is required before clinical translation.

Method

One expert per diagnosis, fused into one decision

2,373 planar thyroid scintigraphy images from five nuclear medicine centers, acquired on different gamma cameras and protocols, were denoised (SwinIR), deblurred (NAFNet), contrast-enhanced (CLAHE), resized to 128×128 pixels, and normalized to [0, 1]. All models were compared under the same patient-level, stratified five-fold cross-validation.

Component 1

Conditional GAN augmentation

A single class-conditional DCGAN, trained on the training folds only, synthesizes new scintigraphy images to balance the four classes. Hinge loss, spectral normalization, differentiable augmentation, and a mode-seeking term keep the synthetic images diverse.

Component 2

Baseline CNNs

DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B0, MobileNetV2, ResNet18, and VGG11, trained under one shared configuration, provide the reference against which the MoE framework is judged.

Component 3

Class-specific Mixture-of-Experts

Four ImageNet-pretrained experts, one per class, are trained as one-versus-rest classifiers. A trainable fusion network combines their positive-class logits with an image embedding, so every expert contributes to every prediction.

Figure 2. Architecture and two-stage training procedure of the mixture-of-experts (MoE) framework. The same 3×128×128 input X obtained by replicating the preprocessed grayscale scintigraphy image across three channels, is presented both to the four class-specific experts and to the fusion network’s own image-feature extractor. Each expert is an ImageNet-pretrained backbone whose original classification layer has been replaced by a single linear layer mapping the db-dimensional pooled feature representation to two logits; its positive-class logit sc(X) indicates how strongly that expert favors its assigned class over the pooled remaining three. The four positive-class logits form the vector v(X) ∈ ℝ4, which is concatenated with the image embedding φb(X) ∈ ℝdb to give u(X) ∈ ℝdb+4, the input to the fusion network’s two-hidden-layer classification head. The predicted class is taken as the argmax of the four-dimensional fusion output o(X). Within each cross-validation fold, training proceeded in two stages: the four experts were first trained independently under a one-versus-rest, two-class cross-entropy objective (Stage 1), after which their parameters were held fixed while the fusion network, comprising both its feature extractor and its classification head, was trained with a four-class cross-entropy loss (Stage 2). A single backbone architecture, DenseNet121, MobileNetV2, or VGG11, supplies all five components of a given pipeline, with embedding dimensions db equal to 1024, 1280, and 4096, respectively; each component was trained with its own independent set of weights.
Results

Comparable accuracy, a decision you can inspect

The VGG11-based MoE reached the highest four-class accuracy (0.676 ± 0.021), on par with the best baseline CNN (DenseNet121, 0.674 ± 0.023), while splitting the decision into four class-specific expert responses. Thyroiditis was the easiest class to separate in every model; normal scintigraphy was the hardest, most often confused with diffuse goiter and tumoral patterns.

Tumoral vs. non-tumoral

Reducing the task to two classes raised accuracy to 0.78–0.80 for all five CNNs. DenseNet121 reached the highest AUC (0.843 ± 0.029) and balanced accuracy (0.776 ± 0.022).

Figure 11. Binary classification performance of the DenseNet121 model for tumoral versus non-tumoral thyroid scintigraphy discrimination. The left panel presents the mean confusion matrix across five-fold cross-validation, with values representing mean classification counts ± standard deviation. The right panel shows the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the two classes, demonstrating the model’s discriminative performance with an AUC of 0.843 for both tumoral and non-tumoral categories.
Conclusion

Takeaway

On a heterogeneous five-center dataset, a class-specific MoE framework matched conventional CNNs in accuracy while making each prediction traceable to four diagnosis-specific experts. The models are intended as decision support rather than a replacement for specialist reading, and independent external and prospective validation is required before clinical use.
